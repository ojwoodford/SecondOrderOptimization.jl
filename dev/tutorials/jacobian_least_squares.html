Least Squares from a Jacobian

Many objectives are sums of squares, $f(x) = \tfrac12 \lVert r(x) \rVert^2$. You can give such a problem to optimize in two ways:

  • as a HessianObjective, whose fdf! forms the gradient $J^\top r$ and the Gauss-Newton Hessian $J^\top J$ itself; or
  • as a JacobianObjective, whose resjac! writes only the residual $r$ and the Jacobian $J$. The optimizer then solves each step by a QR factorization of $J$ and never forms $J^\top J$.

Both give the same iterations, with any iterator. They differ in how accurately each step is solved, and in what that accuracy costs. With a JacobianObjective the model Hessian is $J^\top J$, so iterator = :newton is Gauss-Newton.

The same problem, both ways

Fit $y \approx a\, e^{b t}$ to some data:

using SecondOrderOptimization, LinearAlgebra

t = collect(range(0, 2; length = 25))
y = @. 3.0 * exp(-1.3 * t) + 0.05 * sin(7 * t)

residual(x) = @. x[1] * exp(x[2] * t) - y
cost(x) = 0.5 * sum(abs2, residual(x))

# As a HessianObjective: form g = Jᵀr and H = JᵀJ
function fdf!(g, H, x)
    r = residual(x)
    J = hcat(exp.(x[2] .* t), x[1] .* t .* exp.(x[2] .* t))
    g .= J' * r
    H .= J' * J
    return 0.5 * sum(abs2, r)
end
x1, res1 = optimize([1.0, 0.0], HessianObjective(cost, fdf!; H = zeros(2, 2)))

# As a JacobianObjective: write r and J, and give the number of residuals
function resjac!(r, J, x)
    @. r = x[1] * exp(x[2] * t) - y
    @. J[:, 1] = exp(x[2] * t)
    @. J[:, 2] = x[1] * t * exp(x[2] * t)
    return 0.5 * sum(abs2, r)
end
x2, res2 = optimize([1.0, 0.0], JacobianObjective(cost, resjac!, length(t)))

On a well-conditioned problem like this one the two runs agree: the same damping and the same cost at every iteration, to rounding.

The contract of resjac!(r, J, x):

  • it writes the residual into r (length m) and the Jacobian into J (m × nvars(x), taken with respect to the step of update, so in the tangent space for a manifold variable; see Manifold Optimization and Custom Variable Types), and returns the cost at x;
  • it sets r and J, and does not add to them. They are not zeroed before each call (unlike g and H for fdf!), and the optimizer never writes them. So every entry of r, and every entry of J that resjac! writes at all, must be set on every call. Entries it never writes keep the prototype's values (zeros for an integer m), so a resjac! that writes only the non-zero pattern of J is handed a prototype of zeros;
  • the cost it returns must equal f(x). Trial points are scored with f, never with $\tfrac12 \lVert r \rVert^2$, so f can be any cost whose gradient is $J^\top r$. For a robust cost, for example, resjac! writes the reweighted residual and Jacobian.

J is a Matrix{Float64} prototype, which the optimizer then writes into, or the integer m.

When the Jacobian form is worth it

Forming $J^\top J$ squares the condition number: $\kappa(J^\top J) = \kappa(J)^2$. When $\kappa(J)$ approaches $10^8$, the normal equations have no correct digits left in their weakest directions. Cholesky may still succeed, but on a matrix that rounding has already changed. A QR factorization of $J$ works with $\kappa(J)$ itself.

The test suite checks this on a degree-13 polynomial fit with $\kappa(J) \approx 4 \times 10^9$. With :levenbergmarquardt the Jacobian form reaches the least-squares minimum to rounding in about 20 iterations, and with :dogleg or :newton (Gauss-Newton) in under 10. The normal equations with linsolver = :cholesky are still above the minimum after hundreds of iterations: their steps are only as good as the rounded $J^\top J$.

:levenbergmarquardt_dtd reaches the minimum too in the Jacobian form, in about 20 iterations. Here the smallest squared singular value is about $10^{-19}$ of the diagonal of $J^\top J$, and the damping of Levenberg-Marquardt can fall to what the linear solver resolves (see dampingfloor in the internal linear-system interface): about $\epsilon^2$ times the diagonal for QR of $J$, but only about $\epsilon$ for Cholesky of $J^\top J$, so the normal equations stay above the minimum. Ill-conditioned Jacobians come from polynomial and spline fits in a monomial basis, from parameters of very different scales, and from nearly redundant parameterizations.

When $J$ is well conditioned, the two forms give the same answer and the normal equations are cheaper.

What it costs

For $J$ of size $m \times n$:

Normal equations (HessianObjective)QR of a dense Jacobian (JacobianObjective)
Once per linearizationform $J^\top J$ and $J^\top r$: $O(mn^2)$, in your fdf!Householder QR of $[J\ r]$: $O(mn^2)$, about twice the flops of forming $J^\top J$
Per damping value triedCholesky of $J^\top J + \lambda D$: $O(n^3)$QR of the stacked $2n \times n$ matrix $[R;\ \sqrt{\lambda} D^{1/2}]$: $O(n^3)$, independent of $m$
Storage$n \times n$ (twice that with trial reuse)$J$, plus one $m \times (n + 1)$ scratch copy of $[J\ r]$ for the factorization, plus $2n \times (n + 1)$: about twice the storage of $J$. With trial reuse (callbackchangesstate = false, the default) a second $J$, which shares the scratch: about three times

With a sparse J (a SparseMatrixCSC{Float64, Int} prototype) the system is solved by SuiteSparse's sparse QR (SPQR) of $[J;\ \sqrt{\lambda} D^{1/2}]$. Its column ordering and symbolic analysis are computed once, when the system is built; every damping value tried is a numeric factorization of the whole augmented matrix, so a retry costs as much as the first solve. SPQR allocates its working memory inside each factorization (and frees it again); nothing else allocates in the loop. resjac! must write only the entries the prototype stores, for example through nonzeros(J).

For a dense J, the per-damping cost does not depend on $m$, so the retries with a larger $\lambda$ after a rejected step stay cheap however many residuals there are. An undamped solve on a numerically rank-deficient $J$ (including $m < n$) is reported as not positive definite, just as Cholesky reports singular normal equations: Levenberg-Marquardt raises $\lambda$, and Newton and dogleg shift the diagonal.

SchurBlockJacobian{Float64, SparseMatrixCSC{Float64, Int}} (with BlockLinearSystems.jl)QR elimination, sparse reduced system:auto, :qr (sparse QR of the reduced Jacobian, SPQR); :cholesky (sparse Cholesky of the reduced normal equations, falling back to LDLᵀ), :ldl; :lsmr (with Krylov.jl)

Every row takes every iterator: :levenbergmarquardt, :levenbergmarquardt_dtd, :dogleg, :newton (Gauss-Newton) and :gradientdescent, which uses only the gradient $J^\top r$ and never solves the linear system. Everything else is rejected with an ArgumentError before f or resjac! is called: any other prototype type, element type or index type, and any other linsolver.

With linsolver = :lsmr each damped step is solved iteratively by LSMR on $J$ (dense or sparse): the damping enters as LSMR's regularization parameter and the Marquardt scaling as its preconditioner, so nothing is augmented, factorized or formed, and nothing allocates. Its stopping tolerances are Krylov's defaults, starting values that have not been measured. It suits a large $J$ whose QR factorization is too expensive; it does not detect a numerically rank-deficient $J$. A HessianObjective rejects :lsmr: on the normal equations it would square their condition number again.

Small problems

With a static number of variables (x0 a Number, a StaticVector, or a tuple of them) and a dense $J$ with $m n \le 48$, the optimizer uses a static system: $J$ is an MMatrix, $r$ an MVector, and the QR solves are StaticArrays'. Measured, this is faster than the dense system for those sizes (by up to 30× for one variable) and slower above them. Nothing changes for the caller, except that resjac! must write J by index, as any AbstractMatrix, and with a Matrix prototype the static system works on its own copy of it.

Eliminating variables: QR elimination

When the variables split into retained ones and many small, mutually independent eliminated blocks (each residual involving at most one eliminated block, as for points in bundle adjustment), a SchurBlockJacobian{Float64, RType} prototype from BlockLinearSystems.jl eliminates them by QR, the Jacobian counterpart of the Schur complement (BlockLinearSystems' QR-elimination tutorial explains the layout). Each step factors every eliminated block's rows with its damping, solves the reduced problem in the retained variables and back-substitutes; it never forms $J^\top J$ unless the chosen reduced solver does. RType is the storage of the reduced system, and linsolver its solver: the last two rows of the table above.

resjac! writes the Jacobian blocks and the residuals into the tables of J through BlockLinearSystems' assembly views; its first argument is then an empty vector. An eliminated block with fewer residuals than dimensions is singular without damping, and is reported as the Schur path reports it.

Everything else works as for a HessianObjective: every variable type, including immutable and manifold variables through optimize; both variants of each iterator (callbackchangesstate); callbacks and CostTrajectory; the termination criteria, with gtol applied to $\lVert J^\top r \rVert_\infty$; and an OptimizationWorkspace, with which a repeated optimize! allocates nothing. Time spent in resjac! is counted under gradients in the OptimizationResult.
